How lichen works

lichen

A modern messenger that works off-grid, over stock MeshCore: encrypted chats and groups, voice, photos and files, location and an SOS search. No firmware is touched: lichen lives in the phone, and rides MeshCore packets that repeaters already forward.

§1 · What we use from MeshCore

A message's journey

Everything lichen adds lives in a Rust core on the phone. The companion radio and the repeaters keep their stock firmware; lichen only speaks the companion's documented protocol over Bluetooth or USB.

Direct messages travel as RAW_CUSTOM packets along a known path: repeaters forward them in direct mode without understanding them. Groups travel as GRP_DATA floods, which every repeater relays. The core is IO-free: time and randomness are injected, and the iOS and Android apps and the lichen command line only run it.

§3 and §5 · lichen frame, nonces

A frame, byte by byte

Every lichen frame starts with a 7-byte header in clear. The header is authenticated with the body, and five of its bytes become the nonce.

The body is ChaCha20-Poly1305 with the tag cut to its first 8 bytes: a variant of RFC 8439, kept for airtime with its bounds written down in ADR-0014 (forgery about 2-64 per attempt, and every attempt costs the attacker a transmission). The tag is a counter per session and direction, and attempt numbers the round, so a nonce never repeats under a key. Sessions are never stored: after a restart, a new handshake.

§7 · ACK-on-Error, after RFC 8724

Long messages, one round at a time

A datagram is cut into fragments sent in rounds. The receiver answers a round with a 64-bit bitmap of what it holds, and the next round carries only what is missing.

The fragment that closes a round asks for the ACK, so a lost fragment costs one more small round, not a restart. Hand-offs to the companion are spaced by t_frame × k + 100 ms (k = 1, 3 or 7 with the number of repeaters), because repeaters are half duplex. The animation shows 8 fragments; a datagram can have up to 64.

§6.1 and §6.2 · Outbox, replies, reactions

A messenger first

Chatting off-grid comes first. A message is accepted even while the radio is away, survives a restart, says why it waits, and can be cancelled until its first frame leaves.

What the phone keeps is the intent (the chat, the text, its expiry), never a session, a key, a nonce or a ciphertext: after a restart it is encrypted again under a fresh session, and the receiver drops a copy it already has. It expires after 24 hours. Replies and reactions name the message they answer by an 8-byte reference both phones compute from its author and its bytes, so nothing else travels; a reaction costs about what a short text does. Stickers are animated emoji both phones already ship, and a GIF travels as its GIPHY id only, the receiver building the address itself: one packet each, and no API key. Each phone also records group changes as lines in the conversation: who joined, who left, who removed whom, the new name.

§2 and §4.6 · Phone identity, introduction

Who you are, and how strangers meet

Your lichen identity is a key made on your phone, from a 32-byte seed kept in the Keychain. Its backup is 24 words. The radio's own key only routes: it never encrypts anything of yours.

Two people who have never talked meet with an introduction in three frames, on the Noise XX pattern: identities travel encrypted under ephemeral keys, and each side proves it holds its identity key. Later sessions start with HELLO / HELLO_ACK, with fresh ephemeral keys every time (forward secrecy). Comparing the safety number, 30 digits that are the same on both phones, or scanning a lichen:1: card in person, marks a contact verified. A verified contact who suddenly shows another identity is not adopted: nothing goes to the new key until you accept it. The key's state sits in every conversation's header, one tap from the safety number.

§10 · Groups

Groups: one flood, everyone reads, nobody impersonates

Each member draws a sender key for the group. Keys travel over pairwise sessions, which are authenticated and forward-secret; when people join, the admin relays every key, so newcomers and members never need a session with each other.

A post is encrypted once with the sender's key, signed with the sender's identity, and flooded as GRP_DATA. Holding someone's sender key is not enough to speak for them: the signature is checked before anything moves, which is also why relaying keys through the admin gives it nothing new. When someone leaves, every remaining member draws a new key and sends it only to the others. Every post is flooded twice, because a flood lost at its first hop is lost for everyone. When both copies still collide, members recover it from each other: everyone keeps the last hour of posts, a member that sees a gap in someone's counters asks the author or the admin, and gets the original signed envelope back, checked like any other and shown as "arrived late". A member asked for a post it lost too hands it on once it gets it back itself (every post delivered at 10 % loss per link, 96.0 % without, fake network).

People next to you join by scanning a QR code: it carries the admin's public contact card and a random 16-byte token, valid 10 minutes and revoked when the screen closes, never a key or a group id. A group can start with the admin alone, be renamed, and shows who joined, left or was removed.

§12 · Location

Location: send only when the guess goes wrong

A position is 14 bytes. Sender and receiver both extrapolate the last position along its heading and speed; a new one goes out only when reality drifts more than 50 m from that guess.

Precision is chosen per share: exact, 100 m, 1 km or 10 km. A coarse position is the centre of a fixed grid cell, the same for everyone and every time, never random noise that averaging would remove. Standing still, only a heartbeat goes out: every 15 minutes to a contact, every 30 to a group. An SOS sends the exact position to every share and group every 2 minutes.

§11 · Search

Finding someone who cannot say where they are

An SOS without GPS still sends a short beacon every 2 minutes. Members searching for them measure each one, ping when allowed, share what they measure, and the phone keeps an estimate of where the beacon comes from.

The estimate is a particle filter: thousands of guesses weighed by each reading's signal against a path-loss model the phones fit as they go. It draws a heat map and a 95 % ellipse, and a body bearing (pointing the phone and turning) narrows it. It is secondary to messaging, and it only runs for an SOS or with the group's consent.

§13 · Media and files over LoRa

Voice, photos and files that fit the radio

Voice is Codec2 700C: 28 bits every 40 ms. Photos are AVIF thumbnails of 160 × 120 pixels: a preview of at most 450 bytes goes first, and the full thumbnail only when the recipient asks.

Media go to contacts only, never flooded. Before sending, the core estimates the airtime of the first round; when it would not fit in what is left of the hour, the send is refused with the time it would fit, and nothing is queued. A voice note is listened to before it goes, at the quality chosen for it; a photo opens full screen at the best version on the phone.

Files of any type go too: up to 8 KB in one LoRa datagram, compressed when that helps (a 25 KB GPX track goes as 2.3 KB, 29 s of airtime), with its fragments and airtime shown first; anything larger, a PDF, goes over the Internet bridge below. The receiver opens it with the app for its type; lichen never runs it.

§14 · Internet bridge

When there is Internet, LoRa only carries the receipt

For a full-resolution photo or any file up to 64 MiB, one encrypted LoRa datagram announces it: its BLAKE3 hash, its size, a name, and the keys of whoever offers it. The phone then fetches it over the Internet and drops it unless the hash matches.

The fetch goes over iroh first: QUIC between the phones, through a relay when they cannot see each other, or through relays only when you hide your IP. When iroh cannot reach the sender, it goes over Tor, to an onion service whose key, like the iroh key, is derived from your seed. Sending "via Tor" lists only the onion service, so the recipient never learns your address.

§9 and §13.4 · Airtime and budget

360 seconds an hour, counted by the phone

869.618 MHz sits in the 869.40–869.65 MHz sub-band, where a transmitter may be on air 10 % of the time: 360 seconds per sliding hour. The phone counts its own transmissions and shows what is left before every costly send.

A medium that does not fit is refused, with the time at which it would fit, never quietly queued. Text and positions are never refused: they wait frame by frame for the hour to free up. The preset is read from the radio, never assumed: a 255-byte frame takes 1.51 s at CR 4/5 and 2.31 s at CR 4/8, the French preset.
